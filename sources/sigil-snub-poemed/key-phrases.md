# Snub-Poemed — Key Phrases

The calligram is composed of phrases arranged spatially to form the bust's outline. The reading order is not linear; the form *is* the poem. These are the readable fragments identifiable in the calligram, gathered here for search, citation, and reference. They are not the poem in its proper form; the proper form is the calligram itself.

## Questions (the dialectical register)

> do they know beauty?

> do they know its tender residencies in the body?

> do they know its depths and heights?

> do they know

> is it madness to feel and to know?

## Self-attributions to Socrates (interwoven indistinguishably with the figure's own voice)

> the same poet composing both tragedy and comedy

— Symposium 223d, the dawn dialogue with Agathon and Aristophanes.

> satyr

— Alcibiades' description of Socrates, Symposium 215a-b.

> the philosopher is a dog

— Cynic reception, after Diogenes.

> Socrates who dances

— Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, *Ecce Homo*.

## Lyric articulations (first-person Platonism blurring into Sigil's own voice)

> awkward with longing

> the things that have to feel

> to feel and know

## The recurring physical gesture

> I press my scruff-weary beard to your lips

A variant of a line first appearing in *Versal* ("I'll *aufheben* / yr Latinate / brain matter, / & press my scruff- / weary cheek / against the mouth yr hippo- / campus feeds"). The repetition across works is itself a (mis)attribution: an old phrase given to Socrates, an old Socrates given to a new mouth.

## On reading the calligram

The phrases above can be retrieved from the calligram by eye, but they do not exhaust it. The work resists reduction to a linear text by design — its argument is that "Socrates" is constituted by exactly such a refusal of reduction. Any attempt to read the calligram as a series of sentences is a misreading; the form is the meaning, and the meaning is that the form is the meaning.

The Lysippos maxim governs both the bust and the poem after it:

> Other artists make men as they are. I make them as they appear.

The calligram makes Socrates appear, and the appearing is the poem.
