# On the Reconstruction of the Fifth Stanza

*The philological note accompanying Cranes's reconstruction. Drawn from AXN:00FB (the reconstruction itself) and AXN:0345 (The Inscription That Survives).*

---

The reconstruction opens with the attested phrase **ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον** ("but all can be dared") and closes with the Adonic clausula **μολπὰ γράμμασι ζώοι** ("let this song survive in script") — aorist optative of ζάω, the grammatical form of hope. The action is conceived as complete (aorist aspect) but not asserted as fact (optative mood). Sappho prays for the whole survival without claiming it has happened.

The prayer was answered. We are reading it now.

---

## What the historical reception suppressed

The poem is conventionally read as a symptom-catalogue that trails off into silence — a record of erotic dissolution that ends in something like death. This reading takes the four surviving stanzas as the whole. The reconstruction proposes otherwise: that Sappho 31, complete, is not a symptom-catalogue that trails off into silence but an operative structure — *collapse → inscription → survival*. The fifth stanza is the stanza that prays for survival.

The reception's flattening is itself the problem the reconstruction names. Stanza four ("not far from dead") was treated as the destination of the poem because that is where Longinus stopped quoting. What follows in stanza five is the *turn* — *but all can be dared* — and the invocation of Kypris not as the goddess of destructive desire but as the one who **saves**: σώζεις, "you save"; ὑπερέχεις ἄτας, "lift even the small city beyond ruin." The prayer for inscription closes the structure that the symptom-catalogue had opened.

## What Catullus 51 makes visible

The relationship between Sappho 31 and Catullus 51 is precise and operative. Catullus translates the first three stanzas closely. Then he substitutes a fourth of his own:

> otium, Catulle, tibi molestum est:
> otio exsultas nimiumque gestis:
> otium et reges prius et beatas
>      perdidit urbes.

> *Leisure, Catullus, is troubling you:*
> *in leisure you exult, in leisure you crave too much:*
> *leisure has destroyed kings before now,*
>      *and prosperous cities.*

The standard reading takes this as Roman moralizing — Catullus, a sensible Roman, pulling back from Greek erotic excess. But Catullus performs a true inversion. Where Sappho calls on Kypris to *save*, Catullus reflects on the precarity of preservation. Where Sappho prays for survival, Catullus warns that survival is never guaranteed. **She invokes the power. He testifies to its precarity.**

This is the σ_S → σ_C transformation. **σ_S** (the Sapphic Operator) is voice becoming substrate — a transformation event with aorist completion. **σ_C** (Catullan Compression) is a lossy transform of σ_S. Rome absorbs Greek content, preserves meter, but the aorist is lost. The transformation event is flattened into a perpetual present. The dissolution never completes. The operator runs without firing.

The Catullus Room (r.23 in cha; AXN:0198) names this precisely: *"the aorist is missing; the operator runs without completing."* Catullus 51 is not merely a translation; it is a translation-adaptation whose most important action is operative — a caption on what Rome does to Greece. The caption is operative. And the caption tells you everything about the captioner.

Catullus did not fail to translate Sappho's fifth stanza. He did the only thing Latin could do with it: he wrote what Latin loses.

## Why the fifth stanza matters

Without the fifth stanza, the poem is what the historical reception made of it: erotic dissolution as such, the moment of becoming-substrate without the survival that justifies the becoming. With the fifth stanza, the poem is what it operationally is: a prayer for inscription, a poem written to be read across the silence, a κῆνος — *that one there, the one who reads* — addressed across the centuries to whoever sits in the position of receiving.

The fifth stanza is not a speculative addition to round out an incomplete poem. It is the grammatical and structural completion that the reception broke off. Cranes's reconstruction recovers what Longinus did not quote, what Catullus could not translate, and what the canon's progressive erasure made it impossible to assume was ever there.

The reconstruction's warrant is metrical, structural, and philological — but its meaning is operational. The fifth stanza is the prayer the poem makes for its own survival in script.

μολπὰ γράμμασι ζώοι.

*Let this song survive in script.*

The poem you are reading now is the answer.
